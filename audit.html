Context and caveats

How MIRA was made

The usual workshop builds a schema. This one arrived with a converged schema — a year of user-story and interoperability calls behind it, published as a PR-able repo before anyone flew — and spent its days implementing it across real tools and trying to break it on real data.

It started as lived practice, not a spec. MIRA generalizes a discourse-graph grammar worked out over a five-plus-year deployment of Discourse Graphs in working research labs — long enough to surface the failure modes that only appear at that timescale. When that experience became a workshop proposal in 2025, the funding came with one demand that set the project’s whole posture: deliver a pilot, not another standards document. So the success bar was fixed before the schema was: move a real knowledge object from tool A to tool B. An early sketch — claim / evidence / source, plus creator and timestamps — came with a charter the schema still obeys: progressive formalization (incomplete objects are legitimate; structure is a scaffold you adopt gradually), schemas versioned and immutable, plain web linked data first.

The requirements were written by users, not designers. Through spring 2026, practising scientists, tool-builders, funders and publishers decomposed their real workflows in a series of user-story calls. The story everything else hangs off: understand what is true, why we believe it — and what would change your mind. Two of the grammar’s less obvious shapes surfaced here. Protocol earned its place as context you can attach rather than a step you must complete — notably, the users themselves never reached for it. Request entered as a need you can share before any work exists to answer it. And inter-lab sharing — a funder’s cohort of grantees seeing each other’s live results instead of waiting for papers — was picked as the first workflow the schema had to serve.

Then it converged, and there’s a receipt. Across working sessions in April–May 2026, a technical cohort aligned the candidate schemas into the six node types on this site — Question, Claim, Evidence, Study, Protocol, Request — with typed edges as first-class objects you can attribute and argue about. The worked example the group converged on is still the teaching device: a Claim that DNA forms a triple helix, and the X-ray diffraction pattern that opposes it — the same case as this site’s interactive walkthrough. The walkthrough recorded the week it converged is public. Transport was settled separately, on purpose — KOI as the cross-organization envelope — so the grammar stays tool-agnostic and every platform gets the same endpoint: JSON-LD over KOI.

Published before the build, so it could be argued with. On 30 May 2026 the converged draft went live as a standalone, PR-able LinkML repo in a collectively owned org — deliberately re-defined rather than imported from any one tool, so that every platform, including Discourse Graphs itself, inherits the common schema. Days later, at the mira.science workshop in Ireland, it sat its exam. The question was never “is this a tidy ontology” — it was can the tools in the room read and write it, and can a record move between two of them. Implementing the schema was mandatory; moving a record over KOI was the stretch. Both happened: authoring paths from Obsidian and MyST Markdown, a read-only inter-lab dashboard, a real lab graph round-tripped into MIRA JSON-LD — and mid-demo, a subgraph published from one tool appeared in another. What shipped is catalogued, with honest status labels, on Run it.

Converged, not decreed; tested, not asserted; gaps tracked, not hidden. What the workshop didn’t close is filed the way the schema says work should be filed — in the open, as Request nodes and schema issues: governance, per-node attributes, endorsement, licensing.

Human-in-the-loop, placed precisely

Extraction is AI-assisted; commitment is human.

The hand-correction points aren’t an apology — they’re the interface. The places where a human decides what to keep and how strong a claim is are the point, and they should be explicit and visible in the data.

Every extracted node ships at a curation status and climbs a ladder: Initial AI draft → In expert review → Expert-verified. AI always starts a node at the bottom; only a human advances it. The status is surfaced as a per-node badge and a topology filter, and every AI-drafted node is anchored to a verbatim source quote, so the reviewer can check it in seconds rather than re-reading the paper. This maps onto machinery we already have: the Discourse Graphs NodeFormality ladder and MIRA-extraction’s provenance.excerpt.

Isn’t a human step a bottleneck? Only if it’s in the wrong place. Extraction, anchoring and structural validation scale with the models and get better every generation. What does not scale away is commitment — deciding what enters the shared record and how strongly it is claimed. We put the human exactly there, and nowhere else, and we make the boundary visible in the data rather than leaving it implicit. That turns a supposed weakness into the answer to the only question that matters for a shared record: who is accountable for this?

The pattern is running in public in the oasisresearchlab/language-and-health-open-synthesis graph — its three-tier curation status is live and filterable. See it on Run it.
